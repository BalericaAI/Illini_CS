**University of Illinois (Historical Reconstruction - circa 1998)**

> "Not all knowledge can be written as rules.

---

# Course Description

CS442 examines the theory, mathematics, and engineering of Artificial Neural
Networks.

Building upon symbolic reasoning (CS341), knowledge acquisition (CS342),
pattern recognition (CS346), knowledge-based programming (CS347), and
reasoning under uncertainty (CS349), students investigate learning systems
that construct internal representations directly from data.

Unlike expert systems, neural networks frequently learn relationships that
cannot easily be expressed through explicit rules.

Students examine both the remarkable capabilities and significant limitations
of connectionist approaches while comparing learned representations with
symbolic knowledge.

Emphasis is placed upon understanding neural networks as one component of
Artificial Intelligence rather than as a replacement for previous methods.

---

# Course Objectives

Upon successful completion students should be able to

- Explain the biological inspiration of neural computation.
- Describe the historical evolution of connectionism.
- Implement classical neural network architectures.
- Explain backpropagation mathematically.
- Analyze convergence behavior.
- Evaluate generalization.
- Compare symbolic and connectionist systems.
- Explain distributed representations.
- Identify problems for which neural networks are appropriate.

# Learning Philosophy

Artificial Neural Networks do not eliminate symbolic reasoning.

They provide an alternative method for constructing internal
representations when explicit rules cannot easily be written.

Students should understand both the power and the limitations of learned
representations.

# Modern Perspective

Modern Artificial Intelligence is dominated by neural architectures.

Students should recognize, however, that enterprise AI increasingly combines

- neural learning,
- symbolic reasoning,
- knowledge graphs,
- probabilistic inference,
- and human expertise.

Neural networks therefore represent one important component within a much
broader engineering discipline.
