# CS342 - Computer Inference and Knowledge Acquisition
**University of Illinois (Historical Reconstruction - circa 1994)**
"The Engineering of Human Expertise."

> "Reasoning is only as good as the knowledge upon which it operates."

---

# Course Description

CS342 examines one of the central engineering problems of Artificial
Intelligence:

How can human knowledge be represented, acquired, organized, validated, and
maintained within computational systems?

Students study the transition from formal logical inference to practical
knowledge-based systems by examining methods for acquiring expertise from
domain specialists and transforming that expertise into computational models.

Unlike CS341, which focuses upon reasoning algorithms, CS342 focuses upon the
engineering of knowledge itself.

Topics include knowledge representation, semantic networks, frames,
ontologies, conceptual structures, production rules, uncertainty,
knowledge acquisition, validation, and maintenance.

Students discover that building intelligent systems is not simply a matter of
writing software—it is fundamentally a process of understanding human
expertise.

---

# Course Objectives

Upon successful completion of the course students should be able to

- Explain the purpose of knowledge representation.
- Compare multiple methods of representing knowledge.
- Acquire domain knowledge from subject matter experts.
- Construct semantic networks and frame systems.
- Design production rule knowledge bases.
- Analyze conflicting expert opinions.
- Evaluate the quality of acquired knowledge.
- Explain the Knowledge Acquisition Bottleneck.
- Understand why knowledge engineering became a distinct discipline.

# Learning Philosophy

Knowledge is engineered.

The most sophisticated inference engine cannot compensate for incomplete,
contradictory, or poorly represented knowledge.

Students should understand that the quality of an intelligent system depends
upon the quality of its knowledge long before reasoning begins.

# Modern Perspective

Modern Artificial Intelligence frequently appears to rely entirely upon
machine learning.

In practice, enterprise AI systems continue to depend upon carefully managed
knowledge.

Knowledge graphs, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), enterprise search,
policy engines, metadata management, and AI governance all extend concepts
introduced within Knowledge Engineering during the 1980s and early 1990s.

Students completing CS342 should recognize that modern AI systems remain
fundamentally dependent upon the representation, organization, validation,
and continual refinement of human knowledge.
