# Course Description

CS448 investigates computational models of human cognition and examines how
intelligent behavior emerges through the interaction of perception, memory,
reasoning, learning, and decision making.

Building upon formal reasoning (CS341), knowledge acquisition (CS342),
pattern recognition (CS346), knowledge-based programming (CS347),
reasoning under uncertainty (CS349), and artificial neural networks (CS442),
students explore the design and evaluation of cognitive architectures capable
of representing complex intelligent behavior.

The course examines classical symbolic cognitive models, connectionist
approaches, hybrid cognitive architectures, and computational theories of
human reasoning.

Students compare how different cognitive systems represent knowledge,
learn from experience, solve problems, make decisions, and adapt to changing
environments.

Emphasis is placed upon understanding cognition as an engineering problem
rather than merely a psychological phenomenon.

Throughout the course students investigate the relationship between symbolic
reasoning, statistical learning, memory organization, attention, perception,
and goal-directed behavior.

Students critically evaluate competing models of intelligence while exploring
their implications for modern Artificial Intelligence, autonomous systems,
human-AI collaboration, and enterprise cognitive engineering.

---

# Course Objectives

Upon successful completion students should be able to

- Explain major theories of human cognition.
- Compare symbolic, connectionist, and hybrid cognitive architectures.
- Describe computational models of memory.
- Analyze models of attention and perception.
- Explain problem solving and planning mechanisms.
- Compare human and artificial learning.
- Evaluate cognitive architectures.
- Design simple computational cognitive models.
- Analyze the relationship between cognition and intelligent behavior.
- Discuss the engineering implications of cognitive theory.

# Learning Philosophy

Intelligence does not arise from a single algorithm.

Rather, it emerges through the coordinated interaction of memory,
representation, learning, reasoning, perception, and action.

Students should understand that no single model fully explains intelligence.

Instead, each computational model reveals different aspects of cognition.

# Modern Perspective

Modern AI increasingly combines symbolic reasoning, neural computation,
knowledge graphs, planning, memory systems, and autonomous agents.

Many of these developments can be understood as practical implementations of
ideas originally explored within computational cognitive science.

Students completing CS448 should recognize that cognitive architectures remain
one of the most promising frameworks for integrating diverse forms of
intelligence into coherent enterprise systems.
